# Well-ordering and global order type of real AM defects

**Status: research proof draft; independent mathematical review pending.** Automated evidence is scoped below and does not certify the complete argument.

**Problem.** In §6.2, Question 6.2.1 of his thesis, Harry Altman asks whether D_AM is well-ordered, whether its order type is ω^ω, and whether its slice below each positive integer k has type ω^k. His real defect is d(n)=ℓ_AM(n)−1−log₂(log₂ n), n≥2. It differs from the integer small-step count used elsewhere in this repository. His published work on *addition-only* chains uses a different machine. [Altman's thesis, Question 6.2.1](https://hjaltman.github.io/thesis-FIXED.pdf) · [Altman, addition chains](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tcs.2017.09.037)

**Result derived here.** For monotone positive-integer addition–multiplication chains starting at 1, D_AM is well-ordered in the usual order on the reals and has order type **ω^ω**. The slice D_AM∩[0,1) has type **ω**. For every integer k≥1, D_AM∩[0,k) has type at least ω^k and strictly below ω^ω. The argument on this page alone leaves the sharp slice upper bound for k≥2 open. C14 supplies it with normalized reset parameters. [Sharp slice proof](../am-defect-slices/PROOF.md). This is a written research proof. Lean verification in this repository covers specified arithmetic lemmas, not the entire ordinal argument. We make no claim of publication priority.

## 1. A simpler finite encoding for the order question

The maximum value after r≥1 writes is 2^(2^(r−1)), so d(n)≥0. A bounded real defect gives a bounded integer defect because the integer defect exceeds the real defect by the fractional part of log₂ log₂ n. For a fixed integer defect bound s, the number p of nonsquaring steps in a shortest chain is bounded independently of n. Here is the growth argument again. The normalized head uᵢ=log₂(aᵢ)/2^(i−1) is nonincreasing and at least 2^(−s). Beyond the initial terms a nonsquare is at most aᵢaᵢ₋₁. At the second of two successive nonsquares the most recent two normalized logs are at most the head before the first, even if squares intervened. The second step leaves at most 3/4 of that head. Disjoint pairs of nonsquares therefore bound p in terms of s alone. The 2026 paper gives a sharper bound. [Tarek, Bahig and Anwar](https://doi.org/10.3934/Math.2026667)

For each shortest witness, mark its initial value, its endpoint, every nonsquare result and both operand positions of every nonsquare. There are at most 3p+2 marks. Between marks the only unmarked operations are squares. Specify in finite control the chronological order of the marks, each nonsquare operation and its two references to earlier marks, and whether each boundary ends in a square run or a nonsquare. Specify by a nonnegative integer for each gap how many squares occur in it. This describes both the endpoint and the witness length; for fixed control the latter is a constant plus the sum of gap lengths. A run immediately before a marked nonsquare can end before the nonsquare instruction; if its last result were read as a nonsquare operand it would itself be marked. Zero runs and coincident roles of a mark cause no difficulty. This description has more coordinates than C12 but avoids needing its optimized compression.

**Monotonicity.** Increase one gap length by one. At that gap, the computed head is squared and previously saved values are unchanged. Every saved positive value in the new evaluation is bounded above by the square of its former value. Subsequent ordinary squaring preserves the bound. Multiplication preserves it by equality of the scaling law; addition preserves it since a²+b²≤(a+b)². A marked reference still denotes the corresponding saved value. At the final endpoint n'≤n² while the specified witness length has increased by exactly one. Thus its real *witness* defect is nondecreasing. The comparison is algebraic and also applies when an intermediate parameter choice would cease to be a legal strictly increasing chain. Equivalently compare two valid descriptions directly by inserting their extra squares in succession as positive expressions. On the subset of descriptions encoding shortest chains the value of this function is the actual defect. No assumption that an inflated witness stays shortest has been made.

## 2. Well-order and global upper bound

Fix any bounded defect slice. It is covered by finitely many controls with finitely many parameters. In any infinite sequence of parameter tuples in ℕ^t, there is an infinite coordinatewise nondecreasing subsequence: thin successively in each coordinate, using either a constant infinite subsequence or an increasing one. In an alleged strictly decreasing defect sequence one control occurs infinitely often; monotonicity contradicts the subsequence property. An infinite descending sequence in the whole D would lie below its first element and is excluded too. Thus D is well-ordered.

For completeness, a monotone real-valued image of *any subset* of ℕ^t has order type at most ω^t. Induct on t, including t=0 (one point). Below the value at a tuple a, every representative x has xᵢ<aᵢ for at least one i. The initial segment lies in finitely many coordinate slices, each of order type at most ω^(t−1) by induction. Such a union has type strictly below ω^t. Indeed any finite coloring of ω^t has a color containing a copy of ω^t: partition into ω successive blocks of type ω^(t−1), apply induction in each block and use the infinite pigeonhole principle. This excludes an initial segment of type ω^t and proves the bound for the full image.

A finite union of the control ranges where t≤T has type strictly below ω^(T+1) by the same finite-color argument. Each element of D therefore has an initial segment of type strictly below ω^ω. This gives order type at most ω^ω for D as a whole.

## 3. Matching ordinal lower bound, without an unproved shortestness assumption

Starting from any witnessed endpoint n≥2 of length r, append j≥1 squares and one addition of the initial 1. Its endpoint n^(2^j)+1 has witness length r+j+1. Its witness defect is d*+1−εⱼ, where d*=r−1−log₂ log₂ n and εⱼ>0 decreases strictly to zero. This follows because log(1+1/x)/log x decreases strictly to zero for x=n^(2^j)→∞. Thus each old witnessed defect produces a whole increasing ω-sequence approaching its successor-by-one from below.

Start with n=2 and d*=0. After k rounds the set of witnessed defects below k contains a subset of order type ω^k. For k=1 this is the sequence just described. Given an old subset of type ω^m, m≥1, use only its successor-indexed points, still of type ω^m via the embedding α↦α+1. At each such point d* choose a tail of its child sequence in the open interval between its *original immediate predecessor* plus one and d*+1. These intervals are disjoint in the old order. The chosen children contain ω consecutive points per old index, giving ω·ω^m=ω^(m+1). Using successor indices avoids assuming a positive gap after the supremum of a limit initial segment.

For each point of this witnessed copy of ω^k choose a witnessing chain and color it by the integer difference between its length and the actual shortest length. True defects are nonnegative and witnessed ones are below k, so only the k colors 0,…,k−1 occur. The finite-color lemma supplies a single color containing order type ω^k. Subtracting that fixed integer preserves the order and yields a subset of *actual* D_AM∩[0,k) of that type. Taking all k proves the global lower bound ω^ω. Together with §2 this gives equality.

## 4. The first slice

D_AM∩[0,1) comes from zero- and one-small-step endpoints. Zero contributes 0. Squaring preserves the real defect on each one-defect squaring ray by the published classification and the stability argument in C8. From Theorem 1.2 of Tarek, Bahig and Anwar the remaining cases can be grouped as follows.

- The bases 3 and 27 contribute finitely many values.
- For n=2^(2^a+2^b), a>b, d(n)=1−log₂(1+2^(b−a)). These form an increasing sequence tending to 1 as a−b grows.
- The other nonsquared bases have the form 2^A+2^B (including B=0), with A a power of two and 0≤B<A. For these one-defect numbers d=1−log₂(1+log₂(1+2^(B−A))/A). The correction tends uniformly to zero as A→∞. For bounded A there are finitely many B.

Every subinterval bounded away from 1 therefore contains only finitely many distinct defects, and the second family makes the slice infinite. Its order type is ω. [2026 classification, theorem 1.2](https://doi.org/10.3934/Math.2026667)

# The counting exponent at every fixed AM defect

**Status: research proof draft; independent mathematical review pending.** Automated evidence is scoped below and does not certify the complete argument.

**Claim.** Fix a nonnegative integer s. Among positive integers at complete growth level L, the number whose shortest monotone addition–multiplication chain from 1 has defect exactly s is **Θₛ(L^(2s))** as L→∞. Hence up to an arbitrary cutoff N the count is **Θₛ((log log N)^(2s+1))**. For s≥1 the nonsquare portion of that class at a complete level has size **Θₛ(L^(2s−1))**. The same order counts endpoints that do not have a square root of the same minimum defect. These count distinct integers, not instruction-labelled circuits. All constants can depend on s.

C11 gives a shorter proof for s=2. The new ingredient in the general upper bound is compressing several long square runs into one suffix scaling *between marked reads*. A count of instruction sequences would not justify the same exponent. The maximal-nonsquare majorant used below is also used by Tarek, Bahig and Anwar. Their explicit one-small-step list is unnecessary for this general counting proof. Earlier arithmetic complexity work studies instructions for typically sized integers, a different regime from fixed excess over the growth lower bound. ## Finitely many exceptional instructions

Let 1=a₀<a₁<…<aᵣ realize a witness of defect at most s. Every prefix has defect at most s. For i≥1 put uᵢ=log₂(aᵢ)/2^(i−1). Thus 2^(−s)≤uᵢ≤1, square steps preserve u and every step has uᵢ₊₁≤uᵢ. Use value-canonical squaring as in C8, including 2→4.

The number of nonsquares is bounded in terms of s alone. After index 2 any nonsquare is at most aᵢaᵢ₋₁. Take two successive nonsquares, with any squares in between. At the second the two latest normalized logs are at most the normalized head just before the first (if the nonsquares are consecutive the earlier head equals that bound). The second hence leaves at most 3/4 of that head. Pairing nonsquares shows there are at most 2 floor(s/log₂(4/3))+1 of them after the initial prefix. We need only finiteness of this constant.

Choose a fixed lookback q=s+ceil(log₂(6(s+1))). Treat the initial q writes (or the entire chain if shorter) as bounded data. A later *reset* is any addition, or a nonsquare multiplication using an operand indexed at most i−q before step i+1. In a reset, uᵢ₊₁≤uᵢ/2+2^(−q). For addition use aᵢ₊₁≤2aᵢ and i≥q. For multiplication, the far operand contributes at most 2^(−q−1) after normalization; the operand 1 contributes zero. Thus each reset multiplies u by at most 1/2+1/(6(s+1)). There are at most s resets: with t=s+1, (1+1/(3t))^t ≤ Σ from h=0 to t of 3^(−h) < 2. Consequently t resets would bring the head strictly below 2^(−s).

At least one operand of any later step lies among the most recent s+1 values. If both indices were at most i−s−1, their sum or product could not exceed aᵢ: use the square of the maximal earlier value and the lower bound aᵢ≥2^(2^(i−s−1)). At the late indices under discussion the small base cases do not intervene. In particular a reset has at most one far operand. This fact is needed when counting the locations that a compressed description must preserve.

## Endpoint encoding with at most 2s+1 unbounded integers

Mark the output of every reset and the location of each far operand read by a reset, unless it lies in the fixed prefix. Mark the endpoint as well, merging coincident marks. There are at most 2s+1 marked boundaries. Between successive boundaries all ordinary instructions are squarings or multiplications with both operands among the most recent q values. A reset at the end of an interval is processed separately. If a later reset needs an old value from the middle of a square run, that value is itself a boundary. This prevents the compression from losing it.

For each interval store its bounded number of local nonsquares and their operand offsets, and clip each intervening square run to length q. Record just **one integer** for the interval: the sum of all squares omitted from its runs. To decode the interval, evaluate the clipped word from the current q-value history. If a run had omitted squares, the last q values immediately after this first long run are the clipped values all raised to the same power of two. Every ordinary local multiplication and square commutes with common squaring of the whole history. A second clipped long run contributes its omitted exponent to that same common scale. Induction gives the original last q values at the end of the ordinary part by raising every clipped entry to 2^(total omitted). A clipped run has at least q writes, so a scaled final history contains no earlier, untouched entry. When no run was clipped, the history is already exact.

Now execute the optional terminal reset at its original operands: a bounded backward offset reads from the restored history, while a far reference names a saved marker or an initial prefix index. Save the boundary value as a separate number for future reads. Later rescaling of the rolling history does not rescale these saved numbers. This induction reconstructs the exact endpoint. The finite control records boundary kinds, at most constantly many ordinary nonsquares, near offsets, bounded prefix recipes, clipped square counts and the addresses of marked reads. Its possibilities depend only on s. The construction encodes the endpoint; it need not identify which of several equivalent original square runs contained each omitted square. [Encoder and decoder](../../solved/fixed_defect.py) · [Lean proof for the ordinary-interval compression lemma](../../lean/Compression.lean)

At exact level L and shortest defect s, the length is r=L+s. For fixed finite control, subtract its clipped length from r: this fixes the **sum** of all interval excesses. For t nonempty or empty marked intervals there are Oₛ((r+1)^(t−1)) choices when t≥1. Chains ending in the bounded prefix contribute only finitely many cases. Since t≤2s+1, this proves the Oₛ(L^(2s)) endpoint upper bound. The same argument summed over the finitely many possible lengths bounds the union of all smaller defects.

## Constructive lower bound

For s≥1 choose k and distinct k>i₁>…>iₛ≥0. Reach the tower A=Tₖ=2^(2ᵏ) and add Tᵢ₁. Between each pair of subsequent additions square at least once (h₁,…,hₛ₋₁ positive); at addition j use Tᵢⱼ already present in the tower prefix. End with d≥0 squares. Each addition is a small step. For the first, A<A+Tᵢ₁<A². For a later head m with next level threshold V>m, after h≥1 squares one has V^(2^h)−m^(2^h)≥2m+1>Tᵢⱼ. The level remains unchanged by the next addition. Thus L=k+1+Σhⱼ+d and the witnessed length is L+s.

The first sum is nonsquare. When i₁=0 its 2-adic valuation is odd; otherwise its odd part is one more than an even power of two and lies between consecutive squares. Every subsequent unsquared sum has the form X²+Tᵢⱼ with 0<Tᵢⱼ<2X+1, so it too is nonsquare. Strip exact squares to recover d, take the floor square root of the last unsquared sum and recover the tower addend by subtraction. Strip exact squares from that floor root to recover the preceding nonsquare and hⱼ−1. Repeat back to the first two-term sum, whose bit positions recover k and i₁. Thus all tuples yield different numbers. [Constructors and inverse](../../solved/counting.py)

For each k there are binom(k,s) choices of lower tower positions and binom(L−k−1,s−1) choices of gaps and tail. The latter counts the s−1 strictly positive gaps and one nonnegative tail. By Vandermonde convolution the sum is **binom(L,2s)**. Some of these witnesses for s≥3 may have a shorter chain. At most Oₛ(L^(2s−2)) can do so by the upper bound just proved for smaller defects. This gives the claimed matching lower bound for minimum defect exactly s. For s=0 the unique tower contributes one endpoint per level. Summation through K=λ(N), using complete levels below K for the lower bound and all levels through K for the upper bound, proves the arbitrary-cutoff statement.

## How often a genuinely new squaring ray is needed

The lower family with d=0 gives binom(L−1,2s−1) nonsquares: subtract the tuples with d≥1, which correspond bijectively to level L−1 tuples. Removing the smaller-defect endpoints still leaves Ωₛ(L^(2s−1)) for s≥1.

For the upper bound consider an encoding already using all 2s+1 possible intervals. Its last marker must be the endpoint alone: there are s reset outputs and s distinct far operand marks before it, and a final reset would reduce the total count by one. If the last interval has positive excess, one of its ordinary square runs exceeds q. Remove a square from that run, keeping at least q. All subsequent writes are local multiplications or squares referencing offsets from the most recent q entries. Just after the shortened run the new history consists of square roots of the corresponding old history. The rest of the interval therefore computes the square root of the old endpoint, in one fewer write, preserving strict growth from the unchanged prefix. No saved marker is needed from within this final interval. An endpoint of minimum defect s produced this way has a predecessor square root of exactly defect s: the shorter construction gives at most s and a still smaller defect would contradict minimality by appending a square. For large levels the exceptional n=1 is irrelevant.

Hence an endpoint entering a squaring ray at defect s either uses fewer than 2s+1 intervals, or its last excess is zero. In both cases the fixed-length control has only Oₛ(L^(2s−1)) numerical choices. Every nonsquare enters a ray, so both the nonsquare and ray-entry orders are sharp. In particular all but a Θₛ(1/L) fraction of the level-slice endpoints of defect s are obtained by squaring a same-defect endpoint on the previous level. This does not imply that squaring preserves every individual's minimum defect for s≥3.

The proof concerns the number of different *integers*. Constructing very large endpoints can require very many output bits. Neither the description count nor its asymptotic exponent is a unit-cost guarantee for storing the full integer, finding the best multi-target sharing, or executing under a fixed storage limit.
